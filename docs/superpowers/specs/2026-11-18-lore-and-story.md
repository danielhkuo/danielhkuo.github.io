# Lore and story: the Round

Status: **set aside by the owner on 2026-11-18** as too much world-building ("I just want something simple"). The
story now proposed is in `2026-11-18-a-simple-story.md`. This document is kept for the record: its section 5 (the
wonders that sit nearest their films) and the generator notes in section 4 are still accurate. It was a brainstorm
written by Fable and checked by three Opus readers; nothing in it was built.

- **[Owner]** marks something the owner has already decided, with its date.
- **[Call]** marks a choice of Fable's. The owner can overturn any of them.
- **[Ask]** marks a question for the owner. They are collected in section 7.

## In short

- **The pick is the Round.** An order keeps each work in a house of its own and never two in one place. The dragon
  carries a roll from house to house, and every house writes a line in it.
- **The realm is a year you can fly.** The twelve months are places, January at the top and July at the waist. They
  are the biomes' looping bands, under names everyone already knows.
- **The work comes first.** The Codex opens exactly as it does today. Story is a leaf the visitor may turn, and it
  unlocks only more story.
- **For world generation** it asks for months by latitude, one landmark of each kind, and a few new sights.
- **It comes in three tiers** (section 6). The first is text only, and the owner can stop after any of them.

## Why

The owner's words (2026-11-18): "we were thinking about... making it more almost like a story-driven thing, having,
like, lore... Maybe brainstorm that. That's something that can be done creatively on the side, and this will affect our
layer decisions in world generation as well."

The world has places and no reason for them yet. A story should supply three reasons: why the works stand in separate
towers, why to fly between them, and why to come back. It must do so without putting anything between a recruiter and
a project.

## 1. Four directions

**A. The Round (the pick).** An order once kept every book in one library on a quay, and lost it to a slow sea, a
shelf at a time. Its rule since: nothing under one roof, nothing under one sky, nothing locked. So each work a maker
gives it is lodged in a house of its own, with whoever already keeps a watch there (a lighthouse keeper, a miller, a
bridge warden), and each house stands in a different month of a realm where the months are places. The abbey keeps no
work at all, only the register that says where each one is. Things kept apart need something to go between them: the
dragon, which keeps nothing and carries whatever it is given. The visitor flies it round with a roll, and every house
writes a line in it.

> ¶ The sea took the old library a shelf at a time. Since then nothing here is kept in one place, and something has
> to go round.

**B. The Guidebook.** It is the first summer the realm has taken visitors, and somebody has written the guide. Every
landmark has an entry in the old pocket-guide manner: what to see, when it opens, one dry remark. The works are what
there is to see; the dragon is the hired conveyance and needs no history. Visited entries get a pencilled tick, a
postcard goes in the back pocket, and by the third visit the margins have started to argue with the text. It is the
lightest of the four, and the house voice is already guidebook prose. (From the Opus brainstormer.)

> An abbey on its spur above the river. Open daily; the terminal is in the west range.

**C. The Wander-Years.** Nobody in this realm is called a master who has not first gone away. A journeyman works a
while in each town and leaves one piece behind in its tallest building, where anyone may judge it. The towers hold one
journeyman's pieces. The visitor is the examiner sent round to look at them, and the journal is the wander-book that
each town stamps. It is modest, and true to a builder early in a career.

> No one here is called a master who has not first gone away.

**D. The Unfinished Page.** The world is a book still being made. Where the ink is dry the land is finished; at the
edge of sight it is still underdrawing, which is what the unpainted haze already looks like. The works are its finished
chapters, and the dragon is the red line a rubricator runs through a page to show where to read next. Each visit inks a
little more, and the journal is the book itself.

> Where the ink is dry, the land is finished.

### Which, and why

**[Call] A, the Round.** Five reasons:

1. **It is true to the site.** One register, the works in separate places, something flying between them: that is how
   the site is already built (`content.ts`, six repositories, the register at the abbey). The story names the
   structure; it does not disguise it.
2. **It says nothing false about a real person.** The fiction is about an order, its houses and a realm. C needs a
   life story for the maker (where he worked, and when), and a portfolio cannot afford invented facts beside real ones.
3. **It is modest.** The order keeps anyone's work and ranks nothing. A small Discord bot gets the same shelf as
   anything else, and no tale says a word about the work on it.
4. **Everything on the roadmap gets a reason.** The register, "Kept at the lighthouse", empty landmarks, errands between
   towns, the journal, pennants and marks for finding the way, Continue: each is something the order or the dragon
   would do anyway.
5. **It gives the climates a job.** "Not under one sky" is why the houses are spread from ice to jungle, and
   months-as-places (2.5) gives the biomes session names that need no learning.

**The strongest rival is B.** It is already the house voice, it needs no order, no Rule and no change to the world,
and it cannot be mistaken for anything grand. Against it: it is a frame, not a story. Nothing in it changes, and it
gives world generation nothing to work from, which the owner asked for. If the owner wants almost no lore, B is the one
to take, and its ticks and postcards are worth having either way. A is laid out in tiers (section 6) so that its first
tier costs no more than B would.

C leaves the dragon with nothing to do. D turns every person in the world into a drawing, so no tale has weight; it is
the precious register the owner disliked in the menu; and ink that acts is Witch Hat Atelier's ground.

## 2. The lore bible (for A)

### 2.1 The premise in nine lines

1. The realm is islands, and it goes round: fly south long enough and you come home from the north.
2. The months do not pass here. Each stays in its own place, and you fly through them: January at the top, July at
   the waist, December coming home.
3. An order of keepers once kept every book in one library on a quay.
4. The sea came up slowly for a hundred years (the Long Tide), and took the library a shelf at a time.
5. The Rule since: *not under one roof; not under one sky; kept, not locked.*
6. So every work is lodged in a house of its own, in a month of its own, with someone who already keeps a watch there.
7. The abbey keeps no work. It keeps the register, which says where each one is, and a leaf for each maker.
8. The dragon carries the roll from house to house. It keeps nothing; it is the carrier.
9. A work is kept so that it can be read.

### 2.3 History

**The Low Library.** The realm was always islands, and ships were its roads. Books came by sea, so the order built its
library where the ships came in: on the quay at Carrymouth, three aisles deep.

**The Long Tide.** Then the tide came in and did not go out. It rose about a finger's breadth a year for a hundred
years: the height of a door in all. Nobody drowned and nobody fled. Towns moved uphill a street at a time, and valleys
became sounds. Every few years the order gave up the library's lowest shelf and carried its books to the top. In the
end there was one shelf left above the water, and nowhere higher to put it.

**The last shelf and the Rule.** They carried that shelf up the river, which has been called the Carry ever since, to
the first dry spur, and built the abbey round it. On the flyleaf of the first book up the hill the abbess wrote the
Rule (2.7). It is three lines long.

**The houses.** After that, works were lodged out, one to a house, with people who could be trusted not to leave their
post. Something had to go between them, and there has been a carrier for as long as there has been a roll.

**The Company's age (now).** In living memory came steam, and with it the Company: a railway across the shallows, an
airship that sails the year, seaplanes. It starts things grandly and finishes few of them. Artificers make the
engines. When the first artificer brought one to the abbey, the order had to decide whether to keep it. The Rule says
"work", not "book".

The Tide Office still prints the hour of every tide, and still lists the Long Tide as coming in.

The technology is storybook Europe of about 1900 (trains, seaplanes, airships, clockwork), with medieval church
buildings still in daily use.

### 2.4 Peoples

There are no races, kings or wars. People are told apart by what they keep and where.

- **The order.** Keeps works and the calendar. Offices that appear: the registrar (whose hand the Codex is in), the
  computist at the observatory, the infirmary sisters at the baths, and the walking brother, who is checking the
  register against the world on foot.
- **Hosts.** Not members of the order. A host is anyone who cannot leave a post: a light, a wheel, a bell, a bridge.
  The order builds nothing of its own beyond the abbey. It asks for a shelf.
- **The people of each month** (2.5): ice-cutters in January, gold-washers in March, shepherds and beekeepers in May,
  haymakers and quarrymen in June, the gardeners of July, salt-carriers in September, gall-pickers and paper-makers in
  October, reed-cutters in November.
- **The Company.** A railway company with a charter and large plans.
- **Artificers.** Makers of working things. They have no guild; the order keeps their work.

### 2.5 The year: regions and climates

**[Call] The months are places.** The climate bands are the twelve months, laid round the realm from north to south.
A place stays in its month for ever. That is why the top of the world is ice (it has been January there since before
the Rule, and the snow has had time to become glaciers) and why the waist is jungle (it has been July for so long that
everything has grown as far as it can).

- **Fly south and the year goes forward. Fly north and it runs back. Fly east or west and it stays the month it is.**
- A lap at cruise is 171 s, so a visitor can fly a year in under three minutes. A month is about 850 cells: fourteen
  seconds of flight.
- December meets January at one line. Crossing it southward is the new year, and the roll counts the years flown.
- **High ground looks like January**, whatever month lies below: above the tree line it takes winter's snow and rock.
  The month itself comes from latitude alone.
- **What is rooted stays in its month. What grows has to go round.** Flocks are driven on as they age, geese fly the
  year, and people go to another month for what their own lacks. This is storybook logic, and the lore should enjoy it,
  not explain it.
- Days and tides pass as they do anywhere. Only the season stays put.

This asks one thing of the biomes session that plain latitude would not: the two sides of the loop must differ. Spring
(going down to July) is wet and green; autumn (coming back up) is dry, then bare, then fogged. Each look then appears
once, which is the same rule as for landmarks.

The three accents of the art direction each come from a month: **gold from March, teal from July, vermilion from
September**. The black is October's and the page is April's. The Codex is made of the whole year, which is one more
reason something has to go round.

Three rules for whoever draws or writes this:

- **Colour.** Vermilion and gold stay reserved for the dragon (dragon design), and the palette already enforces it:
  hues from red round to yellow-green are capped at low chroma for land and buildings alike (`RESERVED_SECTOR` in
  `render/palette.ts`). So autumn cannot be told by warm leaves. October is plum heather, bare branches, pale stubble
  and mist; September is bone white and grey-green. Anything truly vermilion in the land (a quarry face, a vane) has
  to be a live sprite.
- **Never a wheel.** A ring of months reads as a zodiac, or as the neopagan wheel of the year. On the page the year is
  a strip or a calendar leaf (3.2).
- **A place's month is written as a place**: "where it is always February". Never a bare "in February" beside a
  project's real dates.

### 2.6 The carrier (the dragon)

The bestiary leaf, as the Codex would print it:

> ¶ **Of the dragon.** It is hatched on the high snow, where nobody goes, and comes down when it is grown. It has no
> wings and needs none: it swims the air as an eel swims a stream. It keeps nothing. Whatever it is given it carries
> to the next door, and there it waits to be given something else. It does not speak, and it is never late.

- **It is an animal**, as a bestiary understands one: not a spirit, not a person, and not a river's creature. The
  copy a visitor reads says "the dragon" or "the carrier". The design documents' word, "river-dragon", stays out of
  that copy, and no tale connects the dragon with the baths (section 5).
- **It is the opposite of the dragon in the stories**, which sits on a hoard. This one cannot keep a thing for a day.
  The order keeps; the dragon carries.
- **Its colour.** The order chose the red stone for its rubric because it matched the dragon. Gold leaf is laid on
  nothing in the Codex but capitals. A house flies a vermilion pennant so the carrier can find it.
- **What it bears.** The roll. A roll lasts one round: when it comes home full, the registrar copies it into the
  register and the carrier is given a clean one. So every visitor starts with a blank roll, and nothing has to have
  gone wrong for the story to begin.
- **How it behaves.** It knows the round, so it glances toward the nearest house it has not called at (roadmap). It
  rests coiled round a house while the line is written (landing design).
- **Its name. [Call]** It has none that callers are told. The register calls it the carrier. **[Ask 7]**
- **The visitor** flies the carrier. The text never says "you are a dragon"; it says "the carrier called at Glim Head".

### 2.7 Why the works are kept in houses

The Rule, in full:

> ¶ Not under one roof.
> ¶ Not under one sky.
> ¶ Kept, not locked.

- **Not under one roof.** One work to a house, so that no one fire or flood can take two.
- **Not under one sky.** No two works in one month, so that whatever a month suffers (January its ice, July its damp,
  September its dust) reaches one work only. The abbey keeps no work, so it may share the home island with Carrymouth.
- **Kept, not locked.** Any caller may read what a house keeps. (The Codex's own link says "Read the source".)

Customs that follow, each of which matches something the site already does:

| Custom | On the site |
|---|---|
| The abbey keeps no work, only the register | The abbey's Codex is About Me, the register and the terminal |
| Every house keeps a shelf, full or not | "No work is kept here yet" |
| The order does not rank what it keeps | Six projects, shown alike |
| The registrar copies the maker's own words, exactly | Descriptions come from GitHub, unedited |
| A house is kept by someone who cannot leave | A lighthouse, a mill, a bridge, a bell tower |

### 2.8 The houses

One of each kind **[Call]** (4.2). Each leaf is at most sixty words. The months below are targets: where the site
lands decides, and a leaf must read true anywhere in its range. **No leaf may read as a comment on the work kept
there**: any project can sit in any house.

| House | Kind | Its months | Host | Its bell |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shelf Abbey | abbey | May | the registrar | the abbey's bells |
| Carrymouth | town | June | the ringers | the hour bell |
| Glim Head Light | lighthouse | December to February | the keeper | the fog bell |
| Tollbell Bridge | bridge | March or April | the warden | the toll bell |
| The Green Nave | roofless cathedral | June to August | the mason | one bell, in the west gable |
| Turnagain Mill | windmill | September or October | the miller | the hopper bell |
| Yewgarth | churchyard yew | October to December | the sexton | the chapel bell |

- **Shelf Abbey.** The mother house, on the first dry spur reached with the last shelf. That shelf is the only one
  here: by the Rule the abbey keeps no work, only the register that says where each one is. A caller who wants the
  maker and not the work is shown his leaf.
- **Carrymouth.** A harbour town that moved uphill a street at a time while the Long Tide came in. The old quay is
  under the new one. The ringers keep the shelf in the bell tower, above the highest line carved on the quay wall.
- **Glim Head Light.** The last lamp before the ice. It has not been out since it was first lit. A keeper cannot leave
  a light, which is why the order asked for a shelf here: whatever is kept at Glim Head is always watched.
- **Tollbell Bridge.** One high arch over a river loud with meltwater, and a chapel the size of a cupboard at the
  middle of it. The toll is one pull on the bell. The warden counts pulls, not coins.
- **The Green Nave.** A great nave whose roof was never raised. The trees have grown up inside it, and their branches
  meet where the vault would have been. The mason keeps the walls. The shelf is in the one room with a ceiling, over
  the west door.
- **Turnagain Mill.** A white mill on a dry ridge, in a wind that does not drop. It grinds the harvest, and between
  harvests the red stone the order's rubric is made from. The shelf is in the cap, under the sails.
- **Yewgarth.** A chapel, a wall, and a yew older than the Rule. It is the one tree here that never sheds. The sexton
  keeps the shelf in the chapel loft, and the key on a nail by the door, where anyone can reach it.

"A roofless nave, open to the sky" (the present caption) still holds. The nave was never roofed; it is not a ruin, so
the realm needs no war to explain it.

### 2.9 The wonders' tales

Wonders keep no work. Each has a tale, and none is the story of the film it nods to: there are no spirits, ghosts or
lost civilisations in this realm, only people with reasons. Proper names are **[Call]**; today's labels can stay.

**The Warm Stair** (the thermal baths; wants snow). A bay on the cold coast that never froze. The order's sisters
built an infirmary over the spring for copyists whose hands had stiffened, and stepped the pools down the cliff, hottest
at the top. Millers come for their backs and ringers for their shoulders, and people from July come to sit in hot water
and look at snow. The funicular is for those who cannot manage the stair.

**The Tide Line and Prospect Halt** (the tide railway). The Company laid a track across the shallows to a deep-water
harbour it meant to build, and named the harbour Prospect. It built the halt first: a lamp, a bench and the name board.
The harbour never came. The charter says a train must run every tide or the foreshore goes back to the parish, so one
runs, with the lampman's oil and his dinner. Its timetable is printed in the tide tables.

**Lower Beck** (the drowned church). When the Long Tide got into the low wells, one valley voted, in its own church,
to dam its own beck so that the towns below would have water. The village moved up the hill a cartload at a time, and
is called Upper Beck. The tower was too tall to move. They left the bell in it, and on a windy day the swell rocks it, and every bird
in the belfry goes up at once.

**The Lazaret** (the hotel in the cove). Built as a fever house, on a rock where ships could be made to wait out
their forty days. The fevers stopped. The sisters had forty beds with a view of the sea and nobody to put in them, so
they took guests, and the rule of the house was never changed: every guest is booked for forty days. People come from
January for the sun. The seaplane brings the letters, and anyone in a hurry.

**The Tide Office** (the marsh house). Since the Long Tide, everything in the realm runs to the tide tables: the
train, the causeways, the ferries. They are worked out here, by one clerk and a brass engine of wheels that has to be
turned by hand all night before each spring tide. That is the lit window. The shutters keep the glare of the flats off
the gauges. At low water a cart comes over the causeway for the new tables, and goes back before the sea does.

**Tarry Head and the *Almanac*** (the airship mast). The *Almanac* is the one ship that sails the whole year. She
goes with the wind from month to month and carries each what it lacks: lemons to January, ice to July. The Company
built her mast on a headland where the wind turns, and there she waits, sometimes a week, for it to turn. Nobody has
found a way to hurry her.

**Overstay** (the bridge town). When the Long Tide was at its worst the river took a market town's low streets, and
the shopkeepers moved onto the bridge until the water went down. It has not gone down. Paper-makers took the arches
for their wheels, and the order's paper has come from Overstay ever since: hold a leaf to the light and the watermark
is a bridge.

**The Ink Oak** (the great oak; wants autumn). The order's black ink is made from oak galls, the hard brown marbles
a wasp raises on a twig, and nearly all of them come off this one tree. The treehouse is the pickers' platform, and the
swing was a picker's chair before the children had it. The parish once agreed to build nothing taller than the oak.
The oak kept growing.

**The Noonmark** (the observatory). The order keeps the calendar, and checks it here, on the highest summit, where it
is January whatever lies below. The brass line across the floor shows noon, and the town clock is set by it, by
letter. On every clear night the dome opens and the computist takes the stars against the book, to find how wrong the
calendar is and by how much. It is always a little wrong.

**The High Orchard** (the floating garden). The register has an entry for an orchard with no house against it, only
the words *not in its place*. Apples are found on islands that have no apple trees. The carrier is sometimes seen
circling something above the cloud. Nobody at the abbey has been high enough to say what.

**The Salt Barge** (the land-ship; wants the drouth). The salt flats are too soft for carts and too dry for boats, so
the salt-carriers built a boat with wheels. She runs under full sail from the pans to the coast with salt and red
stone, and comes back light. She has been aground on water twice.

**The Hours** (the clock automata; Carrymouth). The town clock has twelve carved figures, one for each trade in the
town: a fisher, a baker, a ringer, a rope-maker and eight more. On the hour they file out and round the face. The
clock is set from the Noonmark.

### 2.10 The abbey and the terminal

- **The abbey is the first house and the last.** A round begins and ends there. It stands on its spur above the Carry,
  with a young orchard on the south slope and the riverside hamlet and its bridge below (terrain design 5.6). The old
  cloister orchard, down by the Low Library, is the one the register cannot place (2.9).
- **The chapter house holds the register**, which is the Codex's "Where the work is kept": each work, its house, and
  how far to fly.
- **The maker's leaf** is About Me. Every maker whose work the order keeps has one leaf in the register: name, what he
  builds, and where to write to him. The order adds nothing to it. It is the one leaf not in the registrar's words.
- **The artificer's cell** is the terminal. The order lends each maker a cell at the mother house: a bench, a lamp, and
  in this one an engine that answers when asked. It gives the same answers as the register, faster. The brothers dust
  it and do not touch the keys. The button stays "Open the terminal": the lore names the room, never the control.
- **The bells.** The abbey rings the hours by the visitor's clock (roadmap: bells on the hour).

### 2.11 Names

Original names only. The style **[Call]**:

1. **Plain English, two parts at most**: a thing and a land-word, as old parishes are named. Land-words: holm, ness,
   wick, garth, mere, rigg, combe, by, stow, head, top, mouth, stack.
2. **Named for what a place keeps, gives or does**, never for a person, a battle or a prophecy: the Carry (the river
   the shelf was carried up), Carrymouth, Tollbell, Overstay.
3. **Offices, not names, for people**: the keeper, the miller, the clerk. No character has a personal name.
4. **Months keep their ordinary names.**
5. **A place's name once, then the plain word**: "Glim Head Light", then "the light".
6. **No invented languages, apostrophes or accents**, and none of: tome, grimoire, scriptorium, arcane, rune, sigil,
   spirit, familiar, ancient, legend, keep, reach, haven, shire, dale.

| What | Name |
|---|---|
| The home island, its river, its town, its abbey | Shelf; the Carry; Carrymouth; Shelf Abbey |
| The houses | Glim Head Light, Tollbell Bridge, the Green Nave, Turnagain Mill, Yewgarth |
| The wonders | the Warm Stair, the Tide Line and Prospect Halt, Lower Beck, the Lazaret, the Tide Office, Tarry Head, Overstay, the Ink Oak, the Noonmark, the High Orchard, the Salt Barge, the Hours |
| New places (4.5) | the Low Library, the Red Cut |
| The realm | "the realm", as the site already says. No grander name **[Call]** |

**Other islands** are named by the generator from seeded lists (never `Math.random()`): a first part from what is seen
there, by month, and a land-word, written as two words. Samples: Floe Stack, Auk Holm (January); Goose Mere, Spate
Combe (March); Lamb Garth, Wren Wick (May); Lime Head, Swift Ness (June); Fern Top, Egret Holm (July); Salt Rigg, Thorn
Stow (September); Rook Wick, Sheaf Combe (October); Reed Mere, Hoar Ness (November).

**Checked** against the properties in CLAUDE.md and the nearest others (section 5). A blocklist for the generator:
Greenholm, Swanfleet, Haysend, Coldfells, Marish, Lake-town, Havens, Summer Isles, Winterfell, Candlekeep, Swamp
Bottom, Seafront, the Aerie.

### 2.12 Voice

The Codex's present captions are the model: "A lamp on the headland, lit for passing ships."

- Plain words and concrete nouns. Present tense for places, past for history.
- Small exact numbers: forty days, one shelf, three aisles.
- One dry turn in a tale at most, and never at the expense of a work, its maker, or anyone's faith.
- Nothing is explained by magic, and nothing is narrated doing magic. A marvel is an entry the register cannot place.
- No "thee" or "thou", no "it is said", no "legend tells".

## 3. How the story shows

### 3.1 Six rules

1. **The work first.** The Codex opens as it does today: the same rubric line, name, description, ledger and links.
   No word of lore stands above the links.
2. **Nothing is locked.** Every project can be reached from the first second by roads that pass through no story: the
   text page, the register at the abbey, a `?at=` link, and later the map. Story unlocks only more story.
3. **No invented facts.** The lore says nothing about the maker's life and nothing about the works. It is about
   places. A month is always written as a place (2.5), so it cannot be read as a date.
4. **One source.** Lore lives in one module (say `src/world/lore/`), keyed by landmark kind, wonder and month, never by
   project. A project gets its house's lore through its assignment. Nothing is copied by hand into a project's page.
5. **Short.** Two lines at most in the world, sixty words on a leaf, real text in the dialog (so a screen reader
   reads it).
6. **Never a modal.** Story arrives as a caption, or a leaf the visitor chooses to turn. Esc always flies on.

### 3.2 Where it appears

| Surface | What the visitor sees | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| **The rubric line** | Unchanged: "¶ Kept at the lighthouse" | |
| **The house's leaf** | Under the links, "Turn the leaf": the house's name, "where it is always February", its sixty words, and the visitor's own line ("First called 18 Nov 2026") | 1 |
| **Wonder leaves** | A discovery's caption opens its tale | 1 |
| **The roll** | The discovery log, drawn as one long sheet (3.3) | 1 |
| **Month captions** | Crossing into a month shows one word, as discoveries do: "October." At the line: "A new year." | 2 |
| **The year ribbon** | A thin strip in the margin: twelve marks, a red knot where the carrier is, a pennant at each house's month. It is not a map; it shows north and south only | 2 |
| **Pennants** | A house the carrier has called at flies its vermilion pennant from then on | 3 |
| **Signposts and marks** | Signposts at crossroads give the register in the world ("Tollbell, north, 40 s"). At sea, a line of tall marks with vermilion vanes runs from house to house, each in sight of the next | 3 |
| **Notes and queries** | Below | 3 |

**Illuminated pages.** Each house, wonder and month has one leaf in the Codex style: a rubric line, an illuminated
capital, the tale, and a small inked view of the place from the renders the CLI already makes. A month's leaf is a
calendar page: the month, its look, what it gives the book, and who is found there.

**Notes in the roll (letters and errands). [Call]** Nothing is carried as a separate letter: the roll is the errand.
Under its line a host may add a note for the next house on the round, and landing there shows it. A note is at most
thirty words, signed by office and house, about one small want or one thing sent. There is nothing to accept, hold or
fail. Seven houses make seven notes, and reading them all is one round: every house, in the order that is shortest to
fly.

> For the sexton at Yewgarth. The red stone is ground and sacked. Tell the registrar it is a shade darker this time.
> — the miller, Turnagain

> For the registrar. The count at the bridge stands at nine thousand and four pulls. One was the wind.
> — the warden, Tollbell

**Queries in the margin (the wonders).** The registrar has pencilled a question beside some entries: "Prospect Halt:
is the lamp still lit?" "The orchard: where?" Each points at a wonder, by its month and nothing more. Seeing the wonder
answers it in the roll. It is the walking brother's errand too, on foot, and he is a little further along the road
each time the visitor comes back.

### 3.3 The roll: a journal that unlocks chapters

The roll is the journal of discoveries, drawn as what it is in the story: one long sheet that scrolls, newest line at
the foot. Every landing, wonder, moment, rare sight and month adds a line with its date, in the words the caption
already used. From the Guidebook: a pencilled tick beside each, and a postcard that can be saved (photo mode).

At its head are the chapters. Each is a capital drawn in ink that takes its colour when the chapter opens, and each
opens at one plain event.

| Chapter | Opens at | Tells |
|---|---|---|
| I. The Rule | The first landing | Why nothing is kept together |
| II. The Long Tide | Carrymouth, or the Low Library | The slow sea and the last shelf |
| III. The year | The third month crossed | Months as places |
| IV. The carrier | The new-year line | The bestiary leaf |
| V. What the book is made of | The Ink Oak, Overstay or Turnagain Mill | Gold, teal, vermilion, black, the page |
| VI. The Company | Prospect Halt or Tarry Head | Large plans, half built, still running |
| VII. The round closed | The last house that holds a work | The ending (3.4) |

The foot of the roll keeps a count (houses, months, wonders, sights, years flown), as the roadmap's journal asks. It
appears only there, never over the flight, and nothing waits behind it.

### 3.4 A gentle arc over a few visits

| Visit | What happens |
|---|---|
| **First** (a few minutes) | The flight starts over the abbey, in May. The first landing anywhere writes the first line and opens chapter I. One or two months go by under the carrier |
| **Second** | "Take up the roll" starts at the last house called at. More months, a first wonder, chapters III and IV. The walking brother is further along the road |
| **Third** | The last houses. When every house that holds a work has written its line, the round is closed |
| **After** | The roll keeps growing: wonders, sights, queries. Nothing ends |

A visitor who comes once and lands once still gets a whole small thing: one house, one line, one leaf.

**The ending [Call].** When the last house has written its line, the foot of the roll reads:

> ¶ The round, closed. Seven houses, 18 November 2026.

The roll can be saved as a postcard, and the carrier is given a clean one.

**Bells (optional). [Ask 5]** With the sound work, each house rings its one bell when its line is written, and when
the round closes the abbey answers with all seven, in rounds, highest to lowest. An aside for anyone who rings: seven
bells rung in every order, each order once, is 5,040 changes (7!), which ringers call a peal, and it ends when the
bells "come round". A minute of it would do.

### 3.5 The plain page and the agent files

- **[Call] No lore on `/recruiter`, `/agents.md` or `/llms.txt`**, and no names from it. A project there may gain a
  plain "See it in the world" link (shareable places, roadmap).
- On the branches that have `src/lib/site-content.ts`, the flight's summary says it "has nothing to read that the text
  version lacks". Once tales ship, it should say "nothing about the person or the projects".

### 4.2 Landmarks: one of each

**[Call] Seven houses, each standing once**: the abbey and the six kinds that already have builders (2.8). That is
one house for each of GitHub's six pins. The 10240 world has five each of five kinds; twenty stop being houses.

- **How to cut, cheaply** (from the Opus reader of the generator). Keep finding five candidates of each kind, then keep
  the one whose month suits best. The other four become waysides: the small features of the terrain design (a wayside
  cross, a sheepfold, a jetty), which `featureFor` and `opts.occupied` in `gen/settle.ts` already allow for. Roads and
  hamlets stay as they are, since they are routed to every placed site. Two things are still missing: nothing passes
  `occupied` today, and a wayside must stop offering a landing.
- **Months are targets, scored, never forced.** The ranges in 2.8 are where each house's leaf reads true. A site inside
  its range scores well above one outside, and no two works share a month. A hard rule would reject most seeds.
- **Each kind's siting rule assumes today's cover.** The windmill wants farmable ground below it and the yew open
  ground, which salt flats, fens and forest do not give. The ranges in 2.8 allow for that (the mill at the harvest edge
  of the drouth, the yew before the fens), but each rule needs restating once the months' covers exist.
- **On today's seed 3**, with the abbey set in mid-May: `lighthouse-4` is in February, `bridge-2` in April,
  `cathedral-2` in July and `windmill-2` in September, all inside their ranges. The yew nearest its range is `yew-0`,
  on the last day of September. So the ranges are nearly met by a seed that never aimed at them. The biome world is a
  new world, and no site id or assignment carries over.
- **A seed that cannot place all seven is rejected**, as the terrain design already says of a missing kind. Otherwise a
  project would have no house (it could still be read on the text page and in the register).
- **The round's order** is the shortest loop through the seven houses, starting at the abbey. The register, the notes
  and the marks use it. On today's sites, month order would be about six minutes of flying, with legs from 19 to 82 s;
  the shortest loop is the better order.
- **Which project sits in which house** stays in `landmarks.config.ts`. With one site per kind, the hash is only a
  default, so the owner can place all six. **[Ask 4]**
- **Pace.** Measured on today's world, a place's nearest neighbour is about 20 s away with all 36 places, and about
  22 s with the 16 that would remain (seven houses and nine baked wonders). The world gets emptier, not much wider
  between places. The marks and the ribbon make the gaps a road and not a search.

**Later houses.** A seventh work needs a new builder. Six kinds that would give every month a house, so that twelve
works would fill the year: a pass house under the snow (January), a ferry chapel (April), a dovecote tower (June), a
well tower (August), a tithe barn (October), a beacon chapel (December). None is needed now.

### 4.3 Where the months and biomes go

- **Month of a place** = `12 × ((y − y0) / n mod 1)`: one loop, north to south, cold once and hot once. Height does
  not move the month; high ground blends towards winter's cover (2.5).
- **`y0` is set once, early.** It is chosen so that the abbey stands in mid-May (on today's seed 3, `y0 = −714`). The
  abbey's and the town's candidates do not read cover, but every later site, every wonder and the settle step do, and
  cover will depend on the month. So `y0` is fixed right after the abbey and town are placed, before anything else.
- **Cover can take the month with no new data.** `CoverInput` (`look/contract.ts`) already carries world x and y, and
  a bake worker can read `y0` from the manifest, so a worker still needs only its window. The border noise must repeat
  over `n`.
- **Borders wander** by low-frequency noise, about a third of a month either way, and blend over 100 to 200 cells. An
  island that lies across two months is welcome: the change shows in one frame.
- **The looks can be fewer than twelve.** The story needs these told apart at a glance: deep winter, the thaw, the
  green home look, warm limestone, jungle, the drouth, autumn, fog. The months between are blends.
- **The sea changes too**: floes in January, turquoise in June, mangrove in July, flats in November.
- **Byte identity.** A month term in cover changes every bake. The fixture-world tests, and the promise that worlds of
  6400 and below rebuild byte for byte, both need the year to be a switch that is off for those worlds.
- **Materials.** The shipped files do not grow, but new covers need new materials; 102 of 256 are in use.
- **Landforms that suit a month** can only be a score in the seed search, since the plan stage runs before there is an
  abbey to set `y0` by: fjord highlands and snow cones from December to March; karst in June; calderas as lagoons and
  cones as cloud forest in July; tables as mesas over the salt in September; slate valleys and lake country in October;
  fens in November.
- **Clock seasons. [Ask 3]** The roadmap plans seasons from the visitor's clock (autumn leaves, a moving snow line). If
  months are places, that work is not needed: the clock gives the time of day and the weather, and the place gives the
  season.

## 7. Questions for the owner

1. **The direction.** The Round (A)? Or the Guidebook (B), if you want almost no lore?
2. **The Long Tide.** Is a slow, harmless rising sea the right founding story, or would you prefer no loss at all?
3. **Months as places.** Twelve months round the realm, January at the top and July at the waist. If yes, the clock
   gives time of day and weather only, and the planned clock seasons are dropped.
4. **Seven houses.** One of each kind, with twenty duplicates cut. Do you want to choose which project sits in which
   house?
5. **Bells.** Each house ringing once, and the abbey answering when the round closes. Wanted, with the sound work?
6. **The wonders nearest their films** (section 5): rename the marsh house? Change any of the four pictures?
7. **Names.** Is the style right (Shelf, Carrymouth, Glim Head, Tollbell, the Green Nave, Turnagain, Yewgarth), and
   should the dragon and the realm stay unnamed?
8. **The order.** It is plainly monastic (brothers, sisters, an abbess, a rule) but has no name, no named saint and
   no doctrine. Is that the right weight?
9. **How far to go.** Tier 1 only, or on to the year and the pieces in tier 3?
